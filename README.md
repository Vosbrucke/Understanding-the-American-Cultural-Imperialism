# 1. Introduction

United States in a span of just over two centuries have become a superpower, an empire that is influential no matter where people live. Since its development in 20th century the United States’ impact has been felt across all continents through economic and/or politic involvement. Capitalism under cultural umbrella of US with the free flow of information and free market and the premise of spreading democratic values has found America present in all daily life activities by the means of its products and services. Globalization contributed massively to the pace in which US businesses could expand their international reach. The United States’ spreading influence reaches, however, beyond its boundaries marked by geographic and economic dimensions, and is also transmitted by its cultural means. The combination of the global developments, technological innovation, mainly coming from US, added up to the way American culture was carried through.

Rapid growth and US impact on world affairs made scholars question if the country’s development is not a new form of imperialism, this time with culture at its core. Under such conditions a term “cultural imperialism” was coined. Herbert Schiller (1975), often attributed as the notion’s creator, understood it as “the sum of the processes of pressing, forcing and implementing the dominant layer on peripheral countries”. These processes are supported by, among other, media, which have a special property of social penetration. First carried by press later to follow advancements in radio and TV, media transitioned into digital world of the Internet. In the age of information, it has become the dominant and key medium. Initially as a tool of freedom, after being quickly appropriated and commercialized, it became a tool for promoting ideology. The tools of freedom became tools of control under which US and the western world can dictate its discourse without forcing the public to follow it.

We can also treat the theory of cultural imperialism as an extension of the concept of cultural homogenization, which “rejects the formation of cultural diversity”. The notion of imperialism assumes the domination of the "center" over the "peripheral" countries by controlling the flow of information and through intensive export of goods, including cultural goods, accompanied by propaganda planned in accordance with the theory of marketing and international public relations (Schiller, 1975). This center is defined as dominant states, forming the so-called core, while the periphery is formed by dominated countries. We could state that the key core country that leads the changes is the US along with other members forming the Group of Eight (G8), i.e. Great Britain, Japan, France, Germany, Italy, Canada and Russia.

We need to therefore recognize that the researched notion is not limited to the United States but also relies on other actors’ actions and their presence in the world affairs. The essay’s focus on American impact is dictated by its relatively largest contribution to cultural market and by its arguably leader position in the international relations owning to its economic and political strengths.

Following the given premise, first the essay lays the groundwork for the concepts behind US imperialism. It looks into the different ways how it is interpreted and continues with a review on whether the cultural impact is driven by deliberate actions conducted by US institutions or are a result of unintentional, purely economic interactions. As the paper mainly features how the US imperialism has been carried through culture the author investigates how globalization added up to its momentum while at the same time creating and feeding unique challenges for the period that followed.

Following the narrative, the analysis pays attention to the value the international image of a country bears on the way those challenges are born and mitigated. The third chapter distinguishes what are the elements that contribute to the image a country possesses in a foreign nation. First, the role of a language is examined- English has been a pivotal point in spreading American culture across the globe thanks to the importance of establishing it as a common communication language carried through globalization processes. Next, the paper proceeds with a look into the importance of media in formation of international image. Broadcasting is crucial for the process which was acknowledged as early as in 1912. Afterwards, cinema’s influence is assessed. Last but not least, study of transfer means focuses on the importance of newly emerged field in culture that video games branch is. Despite its, relative to other channels, young age, the video game industry has become the biggest entertainment market with over a billion players around the globe. The heterogeneity of its user base and the raw size has created a disruptive vehicle to transmit US culture. The essay ends with a conclusion of the current understanding of cultural imperialism.

# 2. American cultural imperialism

The genesis of American dominance in the sphere of culture dates back to the post-war period. At that time, Europe plunged into an economic crisis and, equally importantly, a spiritual crisis resulting from the dominance of the Enlightenment and utilitarian thinking, which culminated in two wars, was unable to muster the necessary strength to stop the American dominance in the sphere of politics, economy and culture. Instead of reaching to the roots of its culture, Europe plunged into the abyss of modernism and all its variations with the prefix post at the beginning. This was facilitated by the rivalry between the two powers, the US and the Soviet Union, which effectively blocked the emergence of an independent factor that would remain beyond the influence of one of the superpowers.

The advantage of the USA over Europe in the field of economy and military power allowed the establishment of political hegemony. Going further, economic and political influences also translated into the sphere of culture. If it wasn't for the presence of American soldiers who were hungry for entertainment in their native edition, American popular culture would not find its place in Europe that quickly. The power of the American economy translated into the influence of local companies and creators from the film and publishing industries. Europe succumbed to this influence to a greater or lesser extent, but undoubtedly American culture took root for good, at the same time uprooting national cultures. However, it would not be possible if Europe had not earlier rejected and questioned its own heritage and the foundations on which it was dynamically developing, drawing creatively from Tradition and the achievements of previous generations. The radical break with the past caused by the French Revolution became a reality that the 19th-century conservatives were unable to oppose.

The modernity that emerged after the war was an emanation of American culture in its popular and mass version. Progressive Americanization, as a social phenomenon, went beyond the area of politics or economy. It did not only mean the transfer of American political or economic solutions to Europe, but it was also associated with the impact on the broadly understood material and spiritual culture (Tébar, 2021). Mass culture became the carrier of "American values". Magazines, movies, and music were all American. Also, the space around people was becoming increasingly homogenized due to expansive capitalism.

After the collapse of the Soviet Union and its disintegration, the area of Europe faced a situation where the Cold War division into the West and the East was a thing of the past. On the wave of enthusiasm, theses about the end of history and the victory of democracy and liberal values appeared. Thus, there was a possibility of cultural expansion towards an area that until then had been poorly accessible and was largely isolated from the cultural and social changes taking place in the western part of the continent. Undoubtedly, the fact that of the two powers that competed with each other during the 45 years that elapsed from the end of World War II to the collapse of the USSR, only one remained on the battlefield, and that was the United States, had a positive impact on the attractiveness of American culture. The Cold War conflict took place not only in the military or political field, but also concerned areas such as economy, social relations and, finally, culture.

The end of the Cold War was one of the pivotal points that proved to consolidate the US position. Contrary to previous international order changes, the collapse of USSR only expanded the influence of US and the western powers. The discourse concerning this period often proclaims the event as the New World Order yet, as Ikenberry (2010) pointed out, “the end of the Cold War was not the beginning of a New World Order, but the last gasp in the completion of an old one”. Such a statement does not, however, indicate that massive changes did not occur. In fact, political and economic shifts that naturally followed the new situation and possibilities emerged from the end of the Cold War proved to launch a new era of development for globalization. The term, characterized by rapid expansion of many sorts of global interactions operating on multitude of dimensions became the buzzword describing the new reality that the world was turning toward. Globalization acts on integration of markets for goods, services and factors of production, labour and capital across the globe (Bordo, 2002). The notion is applicable to culture as well amid all the economization processes it has been covered in.

The USA, which emerged victorious from the Cold War with a failure of the Soviet Union’s system and country’s disintegration, effectively used the opportunity to present the so- called "American values" as a universal system that would eventually cover the entire globe. An element of global hegemony was the cultural expansion that followed the dominant power. Such a situation meant imposing on others a system of values and cultural norms foreign to them. American culture dominates in the visual sphere and in cyberspace, it is present in literature and film. Its presence is so embedded in the reality that surrounds us, that a contact with it is considered normal.

It created a situation in which manipulation became a mean of creating a new reality, technological advantage and financial resources that are conducive to the dissemination of content in line with the "American dream" idea. Thus, fulfilling the American dream means that we all read, watch and experience the same thing. Under these circumstances, too, the mindset is American for everyone. This is facilitated not only by the specificity of modern technology, but also by the general infantilization of modern culture, which, instead of provoking a critical look at our reality and forcing us to think, serves only entertainment.

## a. Cultural domination

The original concept of cultural imperialism formulated by Schiller (1975) described the combination of the processes by which a society is “brought into the modern world system and how its dominating stratum is attracted, pressured, forced, and sometimes bribed into shaping social institutions to correspond to, or even promote, the values and structures of the dominating center of the system”. The author claimed that the American cultural influence was born already during the second World War with the birth of the idea of free and unrestricted flow of information. According to its premise media actors could openly express their opinions, make comments, and take part in discussions with no constraints placed on them by the government. As the government has little control over the subjects covered in widely read media, information is free to flow. The supporters of unfettered international communication and information flow emphasize that democracy is primarily promoted by these systems. However, what the free flow of information actually carried was the concept of capitalism. The free flow in fact had an impact on numerous facets of socioeconomic life. In order to prevent others from putting up obstacles to commerce and making it harder to obtain information, media organizations in media-rich nations intended to deter action.

The concept of ‘free flow’ therefore served both economic and political purposes. Media organizations of the media-rich countries could hope to dissuade others from erecting trade barriers to their products or from making it difficult to gather news or make programs on their territories. Their argument drew on premises of democracy, freedom of expression, the media’s role as ‘public watchdog’ and their assumed global relevance. ‘Free flow’ assisted American businesses in advertising and marketing their goods and services in foreign markets, through media vehicles whose information and entertainment products championed the Western way of life and its values of capitalism and individualism.

Supporters of information freedom in the United States substantially profited from the experiences and sentiments of those who had just been freed from fascist-occupied and war- torn countries and areas. However, behind the language of freedom, there were strong economic forces using a clever political and semantic strategy. The issue of free flow of information provided American policy managers with a powerful cultural argument pointing at an alternate form of social organization in form of capitalism. The cultural imperialism would under such definition manifest itself in a way that the recipients of media messages or cultural products in general were completely passive and uncritical towards the images served to them. Most of the prevailing needs of what resting, having fun, behaving and consuming are defined and considered through the “advertised love and hate of what others love and hate” (Marcuse, 1991). The individual usually does not even realize that his or her desires and needs are only a copy of what the broadly understood culture industry imposes on him/her from above.

The other approach to imperialism that interests us can be found in the work of George Steinmetz (2005), who understood imperialism – as opposed to colonialism – as a non- territorial form of domination. In this approach, it takes a veiled form, functioning hegemonically in the spheres of culture, ideology, economy and politics. It is worth paying attention to the ubiquitous processes of masking it, hidden under the concepts of sphere of influence, zone of interest or border revision. Under given definitions we can conclude that the cultural imperialism is non-territorial domination of one country or group over another transferred by products of culture that present and/or promote values and structures of the dominator.

## b. Globalization and the New World Order

The term "globalization" is referred as “the increasingly close international integration processes of markets for goods, services and factors of production, labour and capital” (Bordo, 2002). McLuhan (1962) related the phrase to “the new electronic interdependence” that create the perception that we live in the world of the global village. This phrase alludes to the world's compression as well as to people's growing perception of the planet as an organic whole. Most people view this phenomenon simply through its economic dimension. Yet, the causes and effects of globalization span many different academic fields. Researchers can interpret the term broadly due to its ambiguous meaning, which allows it to encompass a wide range of modernization-related characteristics, such as the spread of Western capitalism, technology, scientific reasoning, beliefs and passions.

cholars points out that the term could be applied to explain the dynamics that shaped the world affairs as early as in 16th century during the Age of Discovery (Taylor, Williamson i Bordo, 2003). Already back then, the time matched characteristics that describe globalization i.e. “growth of international trade in relation to population and income” and a “convergence in the prices of traded commodities” (Bordo, 2002). The author highlights, however, the fact that the major advances occurred only after the Napoleonic wars. Since then global interactions have gone through periods of growth and abrupt downs. The current uptrend wave can be traced back to 1970s when a series of changes on international market started to appear. They ranged from governmental deregulation of financial markets, period of low interest rates to technological advancements in communication availabilities (Allen, 2016). Those factors were formed on a basis of “two underlying and interrelated technological shifts: the greatly increased cost, risk, complexity, and importance of technological development, and dramatic improvements in transportation and communications technology” (Brooks i Wohlforth, 200- 2001).

The next phase that further accelerated the prevalence of globalization took place following the end of the Cold War. The explanation provided by Schiller (1975) of how the depredations of the Nazi occupation had traumatized Europe can be related to the post-soviet countries that similarly were characterized by a restricted movement of information and resources. Freedom of information and movement also in this scenario was the “highly desirable and legitimate aspirations of occupied nations and peoples” (Schiller, 1975). The Iron Curtain not only separated the two spheres of influence, but was also a barrier that for some time hindered the penetration of Western tendencies in the field of culture into the countries on the eastern side of the curtain. With the end of soviet era western culture could finally be shared and viewed. Societies amid the sudden opening of their borders and advancements in communication technologies found that the west is far more developed rendering a conclusion that capitalistic system is far superior over the communistic one. The demise of soviet socialism political and economic system was read as a victory of the US democratic state forms over autocratic ones and of capitalistic market over the planned one (Fukuyama, 1989).

This progress was accelerated by the roaming globalization processes that benefited from new markets openings. The new situation proved to be a fertile ground for American culture. At this point it was carried through additional means- news media, cinema and later with video games which proved to be effective ways to transmit the western principles. Capitalism and consumerism are presented as the norm and viewers may idealize a life that follows. In order to attract transnational viewer the US culture industry also started developing regional, locally relevant media forms with the core values rooted in American ideology. (Mirrlees, 2016).

Globalization advancements in the early 1990s were not universally seen as ways to exert imperialistic power. Tomlinson (1991) stated that the “globalizing modernity” would prove to be the end of the US Empire and would support the interconnection and interdependency across the whole world. The author rejected the idea that globalization and imperialism are linked stating that imperialism was a “purposeful project” while the first term indicates “less coherent and culturally directed” processes.

Thompson (1991) added to the debate pointing out that “cultural diversity is still alive and well despite the growth of globalization which indicates a lack of imperialistic intentions”. In a similar vein Tomlinson (1991) asserted that the content of these media products is invariably altered by the local context in which they are always interpreted. The globalization may have a pluralist impact on identity because global networks simultaneously cause the continuation of earlier social practices and their renunciation, which calls into question the credibility of cultural imperialism. Following this view, international companies create products and services that “appeal to and abide by different national states and cultures” (Mirrlees, 2016). To ground the notion Aysha (2004) coined a term glocalization highlighting the adoption of the process by many corporations. This development, however, is precisely what cultural imperialistic in its essence is. Through the localization of the business organizations can portray their brand’s image as catering to regional needs. Glocalization brings products that do not always appear as agents of Americanization. What those companies introduce is goods homogenization to make them more marketable and easier to imagine in one’s daily life. This state of affairs makes those products do exactly what the above-mentioned authors argued against. With the US cultural commodities being transmitted around the world, even in their glocalized form, they attract consumers and make them consume those and many more goods. Doing so they distribute facets of the American values.

Fukuyama's (1989) famous essay lays a yet further perspective on globalization noting that the “global village” and world peace are linked and will result in the end of warfare and national struggle. From this point, the attained security more than offsets any costs associated with globalization, such as the mediocrity and uniformity of personal lives. The author states that given the widespread fear that most people live with on a daily basis, the enormous benefits of US global hegemony are well worth the sacrifices. Acceptance of this narrative amid the proceeding globalization makes the US culture industry to export precisely the culture of fear (Rowe, 2004). The view is shared across all levels in socioeconomic life and was present also in the increasing US military interventions that Bush administration undertook in order to act as a global policeman. George H.W. Bush expressed this belief by stating that “among the nations of the world, only the United States of America has both the moral standing and the means to back [the New World Order] up” (1991).

Jean Baudrillard’s (1991) short essay The Gulf War Did Not Take Place extends Bush statement on yet another field. There the author presents an idea that what took place during the winter of 1991 in the Persian Gulf was actually a promotional, speculative and virtual conflict. Its function was an extension of corporate, military and cultural control over the alleged victors of the war and a consolidation of US power and position following the end of the Cold war. The outcome enabled the construction of a New World Order of global capitalism and domination, The war was “deceptively staged” and “premodeled” as a promotional campaign (Pfohl, 1997).

To comply with US foreign policy amid Bush actions any mean was desirable and acceptable as long as it helped the country hold its dominant position. Culture industry was among many other means to back the country’s values through transmitting them to other nations. This directs the discussion into yet further area of disagreement among scholars.

## c. Soft power and imperialism discourse

The term ‘soft power’ is used in international relations to denote a political entity's capacity to indirectly influence the actions or interests of another nation. It is different from the ‘hard power’ in the sense that it is the ability to coerce growing from a country’s military and economic power. Soft power strategies relate to directing the public’s view in a way that is beneficial for a country through culture, domestic values and foreign policy. J. Nye (2004), considered to ground the soft-power term in public discourse and its fierce advocate, believes that US needs to step up its efforts in the field by leveraging American broadcast capabilities, increasing the prevalence of US nongovernmental organization across societies. Those elements arguably could be seen as parts of the imperialistic actions.

A debate on whether US renders imperialistic behaviors has been ongoing since its inception as a global powerhouse in the beginning of the 20th century. Some critics argue that though in the past US foreign affair’s actions posed imperialistic sings, it is no longer the case. To be precise US has never fully embraced the idea of imperialism in traditional sense of territorial expansion owing to the stress that military actions, necessary to induce peace and order in controlled land, were not seen favorably in the country. The United States had its brief period in history when it governed over countries in Central America, the Caribbean and the Philippines but it did not last long.

As J. Nye (2004) explains that US instead designs its military “for fighting, rather than for police work”. Such approach is the outcome of efforts that US devoted itself to that only brought mixed results. In the past century US has participated in military actions across all the continents either through direct dispatch of its units or through a joint military action but never with intension to fully and on long basis participate in the region’s affairs.

Following the late 1980s and early 1990s the rise in power made US able to react and induce its power in even more countries without previous political complications. The end of Cold War made the United States eager to present itself as a hegemon that did not lose its guard amid the turmoil. Soon a possibility emerged and US officials took steps instantly with the 1991 Gulf War that broke out soon after the collapse. As J. Hoff (2007) notes “the real goal was the reaffirmation of US strategic power” and its further ability and readiness to act. The move was made to showcase that the collapse of USSR has not changed US attitude and that from now on it is USA that will control the “safety” and the rule of law in the region. The outcome of this conflict left the Middle East as unstable as it was before the war and the freed Kuwait was not transformed into democratic state. Most of the objectives therefore were not achieved and as Saddam Hussein, the president of Irag, remained in power, in turn left the region with severe political tensions that had persisted until the Second Gulf War in 2003 (Hoff, 2007).

The aftermath of the war with the United States being criticized for handling of the war made the importance of pursuing positive image of US crucial. Dispersion of potential hostile groups or states proved to be even bigger concern and reached its peak with the 9/11 terrorist attack. Following the development of global terrorism and hostility toward US, the government decided to show its strength and promptly acted when situation to do so occurred through military intervention in Iran. This sparked massive backlash against the United States and pressured the administration to reconsider the position US occupied in the international foreign policies. With the outbreak of the Second Gulf War many countries were found reluctant to share the burden of conflict with the United States amid public backlash and pressure on governments to withstand participating in the conflict.

J. Nye (2004) writes that “in the age of global information, the attractiveness of the United States will be critical to our ability to achieve the outcomes we want”. The author highlighted therefore the need of improving the United States international image. The elements that construct the state’s image include economic and military dimensions, explained as parts of hard power, and political and cultural areas of influence as soft power. Through their interaction they form an image seen across domestic and abroad societies. The author indicated that the backlash resulting amid the Gulf Wars was an aftermath of worsening soft power and pointed at the need to elevate it back to its previous position.

The soft power indicates therefore deliberate actions undertaken by country officials to promote its positive image for the country’s benefit. This is different from the cultural imperialism which expands the country’s influence through more sophisticated means without a direct input and approval from politicians but rather by the growing presence of the country in global affairs. This development stems down from its economic, politic and cultural advances which all have direct and indirect influence on each other. The cultural imperialism acts across multitude dimensions and has effect on people’s way of seeing the American products, services, political system. Moreover, any imitation to the American life style is going to affect the American economy positively through exportation. The consumption of the American products, which stands most of time to modernity, becomes a fashion in other countries. Under such understanding, soft power focuses mainly on a political level while notion of cultural imperialism incorporates very broad interactions of culture, economy and politics.

## d. Intentionality

A number of critics raise questions against the view that the US indeed exhibit imperialism with its culture. J. Nye (2004) points that US does not intend to expand its territories and that it “must bargain as an equal when Europe acts in a unified way”. This understanding does not respond to the defined previously ways cultural imperialism is transmitted rendering it not applicable. The basis of cultural imperialism is borderless while the Gulf Wars proved that US can act without a homogenous consent.

The other criticism focuses on the fact that the cultural domination is a result of an innocent result of globalization induced by economic relations (Rothkopf, 1997). The author goes as far as claiming that “Americans should not deny the fact that of all the nations in the world, theirs is the most just and the best model for the future”. Rothkopf, among others, create a discourse that “American leadership must depend on the assertion of soft power” (Mirrlees, 2016).

The soft power notion and its establishment in public discourse create therefore a space to talk about its use as a necessity to maintain world peace and order. Culture under such notion is seen as a way to conduct those values. Its transmission, however, is not a product of fully deliberate actions. Scholars debate on what’s the impact of the government induced values through cultural means and what is a result of unintentional purely market based demand and supply interactions. The whole discourse surrounding the soft power was popularized in 1990s amid the changing international landscape following the collapse of the USSR. Resulting changes pushed globalization into its next age bringing alongside capitalism principles. As was already established, under the New World Order the western values were quickly adopted among the post-soviet communities which embarked a new phase for the American culture to thrive in.

The marketing techniques being the main focus of capitalism based economic system enable industries to sell consumer goods which reflect American ideas, tastes, preferences and beliefs therefore actively adding to the transmission mechanism. Schiller (1975) renders media corporations as not being independently cultural or media imperialists but rather being supportive by the “geopolitics of the US”. Companies profit from the capitalism model being adopted by more countries but they do “not act as imperialists in their own right” (Sparks, 2007).

The process of acceptance of the “American values” embodied within capitalism is not, however, fully autonomous and its pace is dependent on various country’s actors to proceed. Those include making calculation of benefits and costs. Reception and acceptance of the cultural exterior influences is a choice made by the country’s decisive groups to include their nation and benefit from the world capitalist economy (Schiller, 1975). Cultural control orchestered by external forces as a paradigm does not capture the essence of how the transmission of cultural imperialism works. It is not an invasion purposely led by a foreign „enemy” but instead often also a decision that rulers follow to enable their nations to benefit from the world capitalistic economy (Schiller, 1975). Instead of a single country, an integrated system of various national sectors dedicated to a particular type of socioeconomic organization is pursuing the cultural and ideological homogenization of the world. It is essential to be aware of the strong, collaborative role of the ruling groups in the dominated areas of the world capitalist economy, in what otherwise appears to be a one-way process of cultural penetration. However, the active, initiating drives from the system's core, specifically from the United States, cannot be viewed as supporting players in the procedure. The global market demands of the multinational corporations under the control of the United States and West Europe, after all, are what drive and structure the global system. The imagery and cultural viewpoints of this centralized ruling sector shape and structure consciousness throughout the entire system.

Blockbuster films, international TV formats, and glocalized lifestyle brands are examples of the post-American cultural forms that the US industry creates. Even though these products do not explicitly represent America to the rest of the world, they nonetheless foster an environment where capitalism and consumerism are viewed as the ideal, if no longer uniquely American, ways of life by viewers (Mirrlees, 2016).

# 3. Transmission means of US cultural imperialism

Having established the notion of cultural imperialism and explained its intricacies we need to investigate and understand different ways through which it is transmitted across the globe. Nowadays, in a global communication enabled by globalization processes, cultural imperialism manifests itself through various media forms from which the most prominent is mass media comprised from television, radio, cinema, newspapers and magazines, web sites and music industry. The other ones, outside of the mass media specification, that we can specify include language and recently added following its rapid expansion- video games industry.

Those means together through their interconnections and the global reach bring the American culture to distant areas projecting the development to be a powerful vessel for disseminating US ideology, values and foreign policy. Capitalistic pursuit of profit makes companies look for new markets outside of the western realm thus carrying the mentioned aspects in their products.

Despite the dominance of Western media and audio-visual companies, the situation is slowly changing. Countries whose economies are described as "emerging" are gradually gaining control over the communication systems in their own societies, further influencing global society. For those who want to gain influence, it is important to master information channels, especially electronic means of information. The next step on the way to eliminating cultural domination are transformations in communication systems within the core countries themselves, which affect the entire system. Following this development, we investigate how the cultural imperialism is transmitted in order to highlight ways how domestic companies can mitigate the western induced influence and act for their own culture’s benefit.

## a. Language

Following the British Empire development in territorial, political and economic power and the emergence of the United States as the global leader after the II World War, English has become the medium of intercultural communication. The popularity of American culture also entailed the expansion of the English language. Scholars often refer to the established international communication language as lingua franca (Todorova, 2018). Across the 20th century the term changed its original meaning referring to French marking a global cultural, social and economic shift that US presided over. Nowadays the term is globally applied to the English language. Societies in non-English-speaking countries adapt their languages to accommodate a new changing social landscape directed by an introduction of new technological advancements with their English naming. In this way, Anglo-Saxon culture exerted its influence on everyday language used across the globe.

The pace in which English gained a status of lingua franca was enabled by economic and politic advances US underwent in 20th century. The Smith’s (1820) quote “in the four quarters of the globe, who reads an American book? Or goes to an American play? or looks at an American picture or statue” referred to the lack of established culture in the United States. Currently the answers, however, would be drastically different than when US was just a mere extension of Europe and its culture was secondary to the original. During this period, European culture dominated on the New Continent and there was no sign that the situation would reverse. In the era of globalization English became an essential language in corporations expanding their business operations to other countries and continents. Many companies have changed their corporate languages to English in efforts to streamline communication and avoid leaving team members “out of the loop” (Johnson, 2009). Robert McCrum (2010) also highlighted the influence of the economic sprawl of international companies on the establishment of English as a new international communication language stating that “thanks to Microsoft, Vodafone, Orange and Apple, this rejuvenated lingua franca has acquired the capacity to zoom through space and time at unprecedented speeds, reaching unprecedented new audiences”. English through such development has quickly gained the status of an international language on a global scale.

American culture could take advantage of the situation by being more accessible than ever before while at the same time gaining from the economic and political advances US market has done. Following the advances US has made the country attracted talented and brave people that wanted to try their luck in realizing the American Dream. Hollywood was seen as one of areas where people could carry it out through. Thanks to it cinema industry has flourished and established itself as a leader making the language even more omnipresent.

Having set up the basis for the importance of language we need to note that English is accepted across societies also because of its utility in international communication. Another lingua franca would exist and be used at the global level if English hasn’t been established as a dominant language. In the end, as A. Johnson (2009) recognizes, “proficiency in English has become a commodity, valuable both because of its utility (...) and its image as ‘a form of cultural capital’”.
While most agree that languages serve as carriers of culture, there is much disagreement over whether the spread and depth in which English is present in non-English speaking countries can be linked to American culture imperialism. Johnson (2009) explains that the global English, i.e. international language of communication found in various non-Anglo- Saxon nations, is in fact separated from its culture of origin in everyday use and that it actually develops cross-cultural dialogue and a nation specific variation based on its own cultural backgrounds. What’s more, the widespread presence of English has raised awareness of the potential threat of overshadowing the local language thus prompting national governments to launch special programs to promote national language or to preserve regional dialect diversity (Todorova, 2018). Yet another aspect that bears on the topic of language imperialism is the notion of English dominance. Debates among scholars point that as much as English is the current global established language there exist regional linguae francae with likes of Arabic, Chinese and Spanish retaining their position and importance in large parts of the globe.

## b. News media market

The US media market in its initial inception phase in early 20th century found itself contained by large entry barriers set by British and French empires. Their colonial system’s media networks fought fiercely to maintain their far-reaching and well established during colonial rules position through which they imposed tight control over their colonies and other nations. Already back in 1934 it was noted that British and French news agencies thanks to the strict control over the news distribution, topic coverage and representation can “present news of their own countries more favorably and without it being contradicted” (Cooper, 1942). In 1922, Walter Lippmann (Public Opinion), pointed out that the media played a key role in creating images in the minds of viewers.

In the age of even more overarching media presence with its immediate access thanks to technological advancements recently conducted by a proliferation of mobile phones and general digitization processes mass media influence societies on a constant basis while at the same time inducing it subtly. Contemporary society, under current socioeconomic conditions with the rapid pace of new groundbreaking technologies like Artificial Intelligence emergence’ or a digitization of all aspects of life, has a feeling and a need to keep informed on world affairs and development. Media addresses this need by making us more knowledgeable, digesting the matter’s complexity so that a reader can grasp the covered topic. Media, as a business operating on economic principles, in search for maximizing its profit looks for most effective ways to attract viewers, readers by content’s creation and new techniques to make it reachable.

Media output’s availability marks a development of a so-called ‘informational society’. It indicates a “specific form of social organization in which information generation, processing, and transmission become the fundamental sources of productivity and power because of new technological conditions emerging in this historical period” (Castells, 1996). The notion refers to the current age in which a world is built around information technologies and values of constant need to gain knowledge and develop. It is different to the previous eras when information in sense of a communication of knowledge was also deemed critical for a nation’s existence yet societies lacked techniques to share it. The technological aspect therefore determines the contemporary society.

When describing the information flood in the contemporary environment, Postman (1993) uses the concept of cultural AIDS (Anti-Information Deficiency Syndrome), i.e. a syndrome of lack of resistance to information. According to the researcher, information becomes particularly dangerous when it is deprived of its purpose or the higher purpose it serves. Information given to us in huge amounts becomes useless and even threatening when we do not know what to do with it. In digital culture, access to information has expanded, more and more content is available to everyone, without any restrictions. This renders a question of what's the point of potentially using thousands of pieces of information if, firstly, we have trouble accessing them due to a constant flow of new information, secondly, we don't really know how to assess the accuracy and reliability of the information we find, and thirdly, we are often unable to effectively use it.

The media theorist H. Innis (1950) claimed that the technology dominant at a given time shapes a number of socio-cultural processes, because the new means of communication are used by the ruling class in the processes of knowledge distribution. The function of the new means of communication, according to the Canadian researcher, is not limited to the simple transmission of information and its consolidation, but redefines the existing knowledge systems. Schiller (1975) considers information medias as the form through which cultural imperialism is mainly channeled. Their ability is to penetrate social media i.e. “means of people’s interactions in which they create, share and/or exchange information and ideas in virtual communities and networks”.

Media inputs initially in the beginning of 20th century transmitted through newspapers and magazines expanded to radio and television to follow the ongoing development into Internet. This brough a new era when supposedly the development of the Internet was about to bring the new freedom of expression thanks to an opportunity for each individual to make its own choice of information sources and social interactions. It seems to us that we have an endless offer of online content to choose from, hypothetically available to us. However, what people actually mainly use is a content offered by Internet giants and large corporate websites. The foundations of the Internet were supposed to be a mechanism thanks to which it would be possible to expand the sphere of discourse and strengthen the processes of democratization of societies. In practice, it quickly turned out that tools initially treated as instruments of freedom were quickly appropriated and commercialized, becoming tools for promoting ideology. A new version of imperialism - media imperialism - is becoming the driving force of globalization due to aggressive online colonization policies and the concentration of capital (Schiller, 1975). Convinced of our illusory ability to exercise control, we legitimize this hegemony through our own online activities (McPhail, 1981). An inherent feature of new technologies that is its interactivity offers us the semblance of agency. This property, however, is nothing more than a technology that reproduces the regime of power. The tools of freedom, whether we want it or not, are transformed into tools of control.

## c. Cinema

Movie industry through the creation of its historical products “shapes and legitimizes our perception of the past” (Kaes, 1990). This way films contribute to a creation and consolidation of a collective memory, i.e. a form shared by number of people and which conveys a collective cultural identity (Assmann, 2010). Memory therefore has become socialized by technology. The sheer mass of historical images transmitted by today's media weakens the link between public memory and personal experience. Filmmakers tend to move and manipulate audience leading the collective perception through a more direct and emotional projections. Historical films need to find balance between portraying facts in sake of storytelling and reference to the spectator’s historical knowledge or memory (Kaes, 1990).

Hollywood motion pictures have always both reflected and contributed to the tenor of their times. No one who saw the American movies depicting World War II conflict, watched even a small number of the hundreds of Cold War films or followed any part of history of politics of the United States could argue on the importance of Hollywood films in creating a climate of fear throughout the United States, encouraging the favourable reception of state security policies and practices.

Film industry ever since its creation has been used as a driver of American propaganda. Already in 1911, the US Army commissioned filmmakers to make a movie The Military Air- Scout set in 1914 about an impeding inter-imperial conflict between the US and European nations. Since then a multiplude of cinema products have been released with and without a direct military or politicians’ contribution. This development has led to creation of a new subgenre of the anti-war movies which intend to represent the full extent of loss and brutality that are immediate consequences of conflicts. Among those movies we can list Apocalypse Now, Full Metal Jacket, The Deer Hunter, or the more recent ones like 1918 or All Quite on the Western Frontier. Despite their alleged anti-war commitment they can justifiably be seen as “examples of a fascination with images of modem warriors, war technology, death, killing, and mass destruction (Hüppauf, 1993).

While those films do address the torture of war and showcase the tragedy that it brings on soldiers fought in various military actions, they also fail to shed a light on what factors and conditions ultimately led to conflict’s escalation. The Vietnam War’s depiction neglects the “capitalistic drive of US war policy, the flawed technocratic outlook of the US power elites that steered the war, the atrocities and crimes perpetrated by US soldiers against the Vietnamese, and the systematic devastation of Vietnam’s habitat by Agent Orange and carpet bombing” (Mirrlees, 2016). The presence of US political interest over movie industry does not relate just to past war themed films. Top Gun is one example with its plot not based on specific conflict or on people’s experiences during one but rather on man’s strive to be the best of the best. The movie’s release coincided in time with the lack of public approval of American military actions following the Vietnam War’s failures. “For the first time in many, many years, you could make a movie that was positive about the military, actors could portray military personnel who were well motivated, well intentioned, and not see their careers suffer as a consequence” (LaRocque, 1997). Following the success of the movie new set of movies were directed this time, however, setting the focal point on portraying US actions necessary to oppose the evil (Mirrlees, 2016). This evil was no longer seen as internal, US actors but Nazi Germany or the Soviet Union.

The movie industry, however, is not driven only by the war themed products. They mark just a part of the whole specter of the other genres. Across them, the US contribution is not directly imposed but American values nonetheless are still present. Such a situation is, following Langley’s (2011) notes not a product of a deliberate propaganda in mind. The vast majority of Hollywood movies “do not seek to directly push specific ideologies”. They do so rather through the depiction of American life and replication of goals and objectives are deemed desirable in society. Cinema products are seen “not just as entertaining cultural objects, but also as shop windows for American goods, lifestyles and values; tools which could be subtly deployed to reinforce patriotism at home and encourage acquiescence abroad” (Langley, 2011). Hollywood’s massive appeal and adoption outside of the United States generated a situation in which audience expectations toward what constitutes an art of cinema changed. Young viewers, upbrought on American blockbuster movies have a different taste which renders them unfavorable of other cinematic styles followed in other parts of the world.

Consequently, Hollywood have proven to be an ideal medium for projecting American ideology. Its industrial strength, worldwide appeal and English being established as lingua franca has made it the perfect outlet for transmission of American values. Hollywood adds to the conviction that America is “exceptional, a special and sacred idea that is more than a mere nation” providing a ground on which US cultural imperialism can further thrive without common discontent or opposition (Langley, 2011). The imperative that the United States is the nation to safeguard the worldwide peace and to promote democracy is therefore directly seen also across the US cinema industry.

## d. Video-games

The worlds of business and entertainment have been significantly impacted by the video game industry. Since their appearance in the early 1970s, video games have become a major industry in the area of American popular culture. With their establishment in culture, video games are currently regarded as a “frequent form of storytelling and representation in the mass media due to their immense popularity and pervasiveness” (Shliakhovchuk i García, 2020). The impact of playing video games on how individuals think and interact across cultures, however, has received, relatively to other cultural means, little attention, despite the enormous popularity of video games, their growing variety, and the diversification of their player population.

The previous part focused on an importance of war themed movies and their portrayal of American values in cinema. In the case of Hollywood the US ideology is transferred also through other genres. Video game industry is different in this regard as it is arguably dominated by a conflict- or gun-oriented gameplay and, if applicable, plot.

A concept of rewriting of militarism and the US war machine through a mean of video- games is presented by F. Gagnon (2010). The author writes that “the accepted way of speaking about an event, an object and identities is constructed through discourse”. Through a process of repetition the representation perpetuates the public ascription of what war, conflict, soldier, threat. Video-games, an industry that’s biggest releases and series are based on war and overall military theme, can give a rise to formation and/or consolidation of narratives favorable to the US. At the same time we also need to take into consideration that the market is very broad. We can find instances where war is presented in a very different shape raising questions about moral aspects and the aftermath of a conflict and a role soldiers have in the terror of war.

Call of Duty franchise, the best-selling shooter game series, arguably adds the most to the common image of a war in video-games. Through various releases developers of the series positioned players in various fictional and historical conflicts. Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 2 (2009) frames war as a “permanent, legitimate and efficient solution to disputes message is clear with such a discourse: diplomace is not an option” (Mirrless, 2016). The series (re)writes the American Manifest Destiny thesis, according to which the nation is a "sacred space providentially selected" (Stephanson, 1995) to carry out a "mission" to advance and defend democracy and American values globally. Following this line of argument, the game also suggests that the US has the duty and the overarching rights to go anywhere and to take part in any conflict to protect those values (Gagnon, 2010).

There are, however, games that do reflect on the player’s experience of and contribution in a conflict. Spec Ops: The Line (2012) concerns itself with the madness of war through the deterioration of Walker and his squad, but it does so without intending to incite pleasure in its audience. Instead, by interrogating the ideas of heroism and responsibility notwithstanding attributed to war’s brutality— especially in its fictional portrayal— the game’s narrative shies away from this reading. The war is never glorified in the game rather pressuring a player to reflect on how conflicts found to be omnipresent in video games. The game, besides its anti- war narrative, includes messages displayed during loading screens that at the beginning of the experience serve as useful tips to help with understanding game mechanics yet later turn into bitter notes: “To kill for yourself is murder. To kill for your government is heroic. To kill for entertainment is harmless” or “the US military does not condone the killing of unarmed combatants. But this isn’t real, so why should you care” (Workoff, 2015).

As cultural products, video games reflect hegemonic values. Living in a capitalist society, we can expect games to reflect values related to acquiring wealth, perseverance, and free markets (Toscano, 2011). Often video games base their gameplay, i.e. the way how player interacts with game’s mechanisms, on character’s development driven by recurring actions or time spent in the game that led to building up skills making player’s character stronger, faster, more powerful to succeed in the game. Citizens are supposed to attend school and/or job training in order to market themselves and advance in a career. Video games, therefore, enact culture by reinforcing the notion that hard work (or just work) allows individuals to succeed. (Toscano, 2011).

# 4. Conclusion

The thesis has demonstrated the notion of cultural imperialism and its presence in the United States across various means of transmission. Media forms have been the major element to conduct American values and ideology outside of the country. Among them news media and cinema render as the most influential areas of exhibiting the positive image of America gaining from news outlets often being the prime reference in other parts of the world and from cinema’s ability to shape a collective memory by meandering between the public interest and business side of movie industry. The newest addition to cultural means – video games – contains many examples of the way it is filled with American ideology and capitalist system. Their effect on players’ perception of the US is still not well understood and inhibits various, conflicting results. The disputes among scholars refer, however, only to the image of the United States often neglecting the fact that cultural means transmit other aspects. The capitalism notion is deeply rooted in gaming industry and is often exhibited as the norm thus potentially rendering this mean as maintaining the current economic system.

The debate over cultural imperialism is still relevant to this point despite half a century that followed since the term had been fully established. American dominance has served as a justification American primacy and is integrated into the country's primary mode of operation and reason for existence. The standard understanding expressed by US seems to be based on a notion that who else could lead the world if not the nation that is the most global, the nation that best exemplifies the "universal" values of freedom, liberty, and democracy, and thus serves as the last, best hope for humanity (Langley, 2011)?

As a result, the native cultures are superseded by the American way of life. Globalization is a crucial force that adds to US dominance proving to be beneficial to the American multinational corporations. With the development of technology the cultural products have become more accessible than ever before. This resulted in a situation when we can gather knowledge from even more sources, making sure that the perspective we maintain is not biased yet the reality proves that currently we are flooded with information making us unable to critically assess it.
Neil Postman (1993) writes explicitly that every technology is in some sense ideologically contaminated, because "in every tool there are certain ideological assumptions, a certain predisposition to construct a world one way rather than another, to value one thing over the another and to enhance one meaning, one ability, one attitude more than another”. The

prevailing outcome the thesis brings is the realization that the world affairs’ understanding is shaped and formed by cultural means that create the basis of people’s behaviours, beliefs and ultimately life values. The cultural homogenization is proceeded by globalization and is marked as a natural step in capitalistic system striving on fostering indifferences that can be achieved only amid standardization of all aspects of human life.
